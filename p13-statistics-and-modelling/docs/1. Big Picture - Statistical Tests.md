## Statistical Tests 

### 1. what is a “statistical test”?

Almost all commonly used tests follow this pattern:
- H₀ (null): “Nothing interesting is happening”
- H₁ (alternative): “There is an effect / difference / relationship”
- You feed in:
  - A sample (returns, conversions, complaints, etc.)
  - Assumptions (normality, independence, etc.)
- The test gives you:
  - A test statistic
  - A p-value → if p < α (say 0.05), you reject H₀
In finance terms: “Is this strategy really beating the benchmark, or is it just noise?”

### 2. Main families of statistical tests
I’ll group them by the type of question and typical wealth/brokerage use-cases.

#### A. Tests on means (location):  “Is the average different?”
Common tests

1. One-sample t-test / z-test

- Question: Is the mean of my sample different from a known value?
- H₀: μ = μ₀ (no difference)
- Use when: sample mean vs benchmark.

Wealth/Brokerage use-cases
- Does a strategy’s average daily return differ from 0 / risk-free rate?
- Is client portfolio performance statistically better/worse than a benchmark (e.g., NIFTY 50, S&P 500)?
- Is average order execution time above a defined SLA?

Conclusion
- Reject H₀ → “The average is statistically different from μ₀ (e.g., there is positive alpha).”
- Fail to reject → “No statistically detectable difference; performance may be just noise.”

2. Two-sample t-test (independent)  

- Question: Are the means of two groups different?
- H₀: μ₁ = μ₂
- Use when: two different sets of clients, periods, or strategies.

Use-cases
- Compare average returns of Strategy A vs Strategy B.
- Compare average revenue per client pre- vs post-launch of a new advisory tool across different client cohorts.
- Compare average margin / trading volume of clients using the mobile app vs only using web.

Conclusion
- Reject H₀ → “There is evidence that the two groups differ in their average (return, revenue, etc.).”

3. Paired t-test
   - Question: Are paired observations (before/after on same entity) different in mean?
   - H₀: Mean of (After – Before) = 0
   - Use when: same clients before & after a change.

   Use-cases
   - Pre vs post-migration to a new order execution engine: latency per client.
   - Before vs after rollout of robo-advisory: changes in clients’ portfolio turnover or risk levels.
   - Effect of nudges: did personalized alerts reduce instances of margin calls for the same set of clients?

   Conclusion
   - Reject H₀ → “There is a statistically significant average change after the intervention.”

4. ANOVA (Analysis of Variance)
- Question: Are there differences in means across >2 groups?
- H₀: μ₁ = μ₂ = … = μₖ
- Use when: comparing multiple strategies, segments, or time-buckets.

Use-cases
- Compare average annual returns across 4 risk models (Conservative, Balanced, Aggressive, Very Aggressive).
- Compare average revenue/trades per client across wealth tiers (Mass Affluent, HNI, UHNI).
- Compare mean client satisfaction across multiple RM teams or branches.

Conclusion
- Reject H₀ → “At least one group mean differs; follow-up with post-hoc tests (Tukey, etc.) to see which ones.”   

#### B. Tests on variance (spread / volatility): “Is the risk/volatility different?”
1. F-test / Levene’s test / Bartlett’s test
   - Question: Are the variances (volatilities) of two or more groups different?
   - H₀: σ₁² = σ₂² (or all equal for multiple groups)
   - Use when: you care about risk (not just return).

   Use-cases
   - Compare volatility of Strategy A vs Strategy B: same average return but different risk profiles.
   - Check if client portfolio volatility differs by segment (e.g., advisory vs self-directed).
   - Evaluate whether new execution logic has reduced variability in order fill times.

   Conclusion
   - Reject H₀ → “Variance is significantly different; risk/volatility profiles differ.”

#### C. Tests on proportions: “Is this rate different?”
1. Z-test for proportions / Two-proportion test
   - Question: Are proportions / rates different from a benchmark or between groups?
   - H₀: p = p₀ or p₁ = p₂
   Use-cases
   - Did the conversion rate (visits → account opened, or accounts → funded) change after a new onboarding flow?
   - Are margin call occurrence rates different across client risk categories?
   - Is the fraction of clients opting into advisory higher in a new campaign vs old?
   Conclusion
   - Reject H₀ → “The proportion/rate is significantly different (improved or worsened).”
2. Chi-square test for goodness-of-fit / proportions
   - Question: Do observed counts match expected distribution?
   - H₀: observed distribution = expected distribution
   Use-cases
   - Are trades by asset class (equities, ETFs, derivatives, mutual funds) in line with the target/expected mix?
   - Are complaint categories distributed as expected, or is there a spike in a specific category (e.g., execution, statements, fees)?
   - Is the distribution of client risk scores stable over time, or skewing toward higher risk?
   Conclusion
   - Reject H₀ → “Observed pattern of proportions deviates from expectation → something changed in client behaviour or system performance.”

#### D. Tests of independence / association: “Are these two variables related?”
1. Chi-square test of independence
   - Question: Are two categorical variables independent?
   - H₀: no association between variables.
   Use-cases
   - Relationship between client risk category (Conservative / Moderate / Aggressive) and product usage (Funds / Direct Equity / Derivatives).
   - Association between segment (Retail / HNI / Ultra) and probability of churn.
   - Is RM coverage (Dedicated RM vs Pooled RM) associated with complaint incidence?
   Conclusion
   - Reject H₀ → “There is a statistical association; client segment does relate to product mix / churn / complaints etc.”

2. Fisher’s Exact Test
- Same concept as chi-square but for small sample sizes or when expected counts are low.
Use-case
- Investigating association between a rare product (e.g., structured notes) and a specific outcome (e.g., early redemption complaints) in a small pilot cohort.

#### E. Correlation tests: “Is there a monotonic/linear relationship?”

1. Pearson correlation test
   - Question: Is there a linear relationship between two continuous variables?
   - H₀: ρ = 0 (no linear correlation).
   Use-cases
   - Correlation between client risk scores and portfolio volatility.
   - Correlation between client wealth and advisory fee revenue.
   - Correlation between market volatility (VIX, India VIX) and intraday trading volumes.
   Conclusion
   - Reject H₀ → “There is significant linear correlation (positive or negative).”
2. Spearman rank correlation / Kendall’s tau
   - Question: Is there a monotonic (not necessarily linear) relationship?
   - Use when: data not normal, outliers, or ordinal scales.
   Use-cases
   - Does analyst rating rank (Strong Buy, Buy, Hold,…) correlate with subsequent stock returns ranking?
   - Do RMs ranked by client satisfaction have higher NNA (Net New Assets) rankings?
   Conclusion
   - Reject H₀ → “There is a significant monotonic relationship between ranks.”

#### F. Regression-related tests: “Does this factor matter?”

1. t-tests on regression coefficients
   - Question: Is a given predictor (factor) significantly different from zero in a regression model?
   - H₀: βᵢ = 0
   Use-cases
   - Factor models: is there statistically significant alpha after controlling for market, size, value, etc.?
   - Does RM contact frequency significantly impact client NNA?
   - Does push-notification engagement significantly influence trade frequency or app logins?
   Conclusion
   - Reject H₀ for βᵢ → “This factor contributes meaningfully to the outcome, controlling for others.”

2. Overall F-test for regression
   - Question: Does the model as a whole explain more variance than a null model?
   - H₀: All βᵢ = 0 (no predictors matter).
   Use-cases
   - Does your client churn model explain churn beyond chance?
   - Does a multi-factor performance model explain variation in portfolio returns?
   Conclusion
   - Reject H₀ → “The model has explanatory power overall.”

#### G. Non-parametric tests: “When normality assumptions break”

Used when:
- Data are non-normal (heavy-tailed, skewed; very common with returns).
- There are outliers or small samples.

##### 1. Wilcoxon signed-rank test (paired)
   - Non-parametric equivalent of paired t-test.
   Use-cases
   - Evaluate median difference in bid-ask spreads before/after a microstructure change.
   - Median change in client portfolio drawdowns after new risk controls.

##### 2. Mann–Whitney U / Wilcoxon rank-sum (unpaired)
   - Non-parametric equivalent of two-sample t-test.
   Use-cases
   - Compare return distribution of two trading strategies without assuming normality.
   - Compare median order size for clients on different pricing plans.

##### 3. Kruskal–Wallis test
   - Non-parametric equivalent of one-way ANOVA (>2 groups).
   Use-cases
   - Compare median daily P&L across multiple trading desks without assuming normality.
Conclusions for all of these
- Reject H₀ → “There is a difference in distributions (often interpreted as medians) across groups.”

#### H. Goodness-of-fit / distribution tests: “Is my distribution assumption reasonable?”

##### 1. Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K-S) test / Anderson–Darling
   - Question: Does the empirical distribution match a specified theoretical distribution (e.g., Normal)?
   - H₀: Data follow the specified distribution.
   Use-cases
   - Check if log returns of a portfolio are approximately normal (for VaR / risk models).
   - Validate if inter-trade times match assumed distribution for simulation.

##### 2. Jarque–Bera test
   - Specifically tests normality based on skewness & kurtosis.
   Use-cases
   - Validate assumptions of risk / pricing models (many older models assumed normal returns).
   - Show statistically that returns are fat-tailed → justifying using more robust risk measures (e.g., Expected Shortfall).
   Conclusion
   - Reject H₀ → “The distribution assumption (e.g., normality) is not supported; model assumptions may be invalid.”

#### I. Time-series tests (very relevant in markets)
1. Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test
   - Question: Does a time series have a unit root (is it non-stationary)?
   - H₀: Series has a unit root (non-stationary).
   Use-cases
   - Test if a price series is non-stationary vs log-returns, which should be closer to stationary.
   - Check stationarity of factor time-series (e.g., spreads, yield curves) before building ARIMA, VAR models.
   Conclusion
   - Reject H₀ → “Series is stationary (no unit root).”
   - Fail to reject → treat as non-stationary (difference, use returns, etc.).
2. KPSS test
   - Complement to ADF:
     - H₀: Series is stationary.
   Use-case
   - Joint use with ADF to more robustly infer stationarity of returns, spreads, etc.
3. Ljung–Box / Portmanteau tests
   - Question: Is there autocorrelation in a time series (or residuals) at various lags?
   - H₀: No autocorrelation up to lag k.
   Use-cases
   - Check if strategy P&L residuals after a model are close to white noise.
   - Validate if risk model residuals or forecast errors are uncorrelated.
   Conclusion
   - Reject H₀ → “There is residual structure/autocorrelation → model may be misspecified or underfitting.”

### 3. How these tests are actually used in wealth & brokerage
At a higher level, these tests support decisions in a few key themes:
1. Strategy evaluation & product governance
   - t-tests / ANOVA / non-parametric tests → “Is this strategy or advisory model truly better than benchmark/alternatives?”
   - Volatility/variance tests → “Is the risk profile materially different from what we promised?”
   - Goodness-of-fit / distribution tests → “Are our VaR or stress models built on valid assumptions?”
2. Client analytics & segmentation
   - Proportion tests & chi-square → “Do different client segments behave differently (conversion, adoption, churn)?”
   - Correlation & regression tests → “Which factors explain NNA, trading frequency, product uptake?”
   - Independence tests → “Are risk categories and product choices related?”
3. Operational performance & UX
   - Paired t-tests / Wilcoxon → “Did system changes improve latency, error rates, or experience metrics?”
   - Proportion tests → “Did the new onboarding flow improve completion rates without increasing drop-offs elsewhere?”
4. Risk & compliance
   - Time-series tests (ADF, Ljung–Box) → “Are our risk factor models well-behaved and correctly specified?”
   - Proportion / chi-square tests → “Are certain complaint types over-represented, indicating a systemic issue?”
   - Association tests → “Is there a relationship between certain products and complaints / mis-selling risk?”

### 4. What conclusions can you credibly draw?
From a principal ML / quant / data role, you’d typically frame conclusions like:
- Significance: “The improvement is statistically significant at the 5% level.”
- Direction & magnitude: “Average daily return increased by 15 bps; 95% CI [10, 20 bps].”
- Practical relevance: “The effect size is moderate; this translates to ₹X more revenue per client per year.”
- Model validity: “Model residuals show no significant autocorrelation (Ljung–Box p > 0.1); normality is rejected (JB test), but tails are well handled by our chosen loss function.”
- Risk implications: “Portfolio B offers the same average return but has significantly lower volatility (Levene’s test p < 0.01), making it preferable for conservative clients.”
